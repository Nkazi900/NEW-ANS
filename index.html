What is the autonomic nervous system?

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is a division of the peripheral nervous system that regulates involuntary physiological processes — including heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, digestion, and pupil size — without conscious control. It works continuously to maintain homeostasis, often in concert with hormonal systems.

Key structural features

Two-neuron chain

All ANS pathways use a preganglionic neuron (CNS → ganglion) and a postganglionic neuron (ganglion → effector). This contrasts with somatic motor neurons, which synapse directly on skeletal muscle without a ganglion relay.

Ganglia

Ganglia are clusters of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS. Sympathetic ganglia lie close to the spinal cord (paravertebral chain). Parasympathetic ganglia are found near or within target organs (terminal ganglia).

Dual innervation

Most visceral organs receive input from both divisions with opposing effects. This push-pull arrangement allows precise homeostatic control — e.g. sympathetic raises heart rate while parasympathetic lowers it.

Autonomic tone

Both divisions maintain a baseline firing rate (tone). Dominant parasympathetic tone at the SA node sets resting heart rate ~70 bpm. Increasing sympathetic tone or withdrawing vagal tone raises heart rate.

The ANS has two principal divisions with broadly opposing functions. Understanding their anatomical origins and fibre lengths is key to predicting drug effects and clinical presentations.

Parasympathetic Rest & digest

Origin: Craniosacral — cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X (vagus) and sacral cord S2–S4.

Ganglia: Terminal ganglia, near or within target organs. Very short postganglionic fibres.

Effects: ↓ Heart rate, ↑ GI motility & secretion, bronchoconstriction, pupil constriction (miosis), bladder contraction.

Sympathetic Fight or flight

Origin: Thoracolumbar — lateral horns of spinal cord T1–L2.

Ganglia: Paravertebral chain ganglia close to spinal cord. Long postganglionic fibres.

Effects: ↑ Heart rate & contractility, vasoconstriction (skin, viscera), bronchodilation, ↑ blood glucose, pupil dilation (mydriasis), sweating.

Adrenal medulla — special case: Innervated by sympathetic preganglionic fibres directly (no postganglionic neuron). Chromaffin cells act as modified neurons, secreting epinephrine (~80%) and norepinephrine (~20%) directly into the bloodstream — amplifying the sympathetic response systemically.

Neurotransmitter identity depends on the division and whether the neuron is preganglionic or postganglionic. The receptor subtype on the target cell determines the downstream cellular response via a specific second-messenger pathway.

Clinical pharmacology: Beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol) block β₁ → ↓ heart rate and contractility — used in hypertension and heart failure. Salbutamol activates β₂ selectively → bronchodilation in asthma. Prazosin blocks α₁ → vasodilation for hypertension. Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors → ↑ heart rate (used in bradycardia and organophosphate poisoning).

When you move from lying to standing, approximately 500 ml of blood shifts downwards by gravity into the lower limbs and splanchnic circulation. This transiently reduces venous return and cardiac output. The ANS must correct this rapidly via the baroreflex to prevent dizziness or fainting.

The baroreflex arc

Orthostatic hypotension

When the baroreflex fails to fully compensate for postural change, a sustained fall in blood pressure of ≥20 mmHg systolic (or ≥10 mmHg diastolic) within 3 minutes of standing constitutes orthostatic (postural) hypotension.

Common causes

Dehydration / hypovolaemia · Autonomic neuropathy (diabetes, Parkinson's disease) · Medications (antihypertensives, diuretics, tricyclic antidepressants, alpha-blockers) · Prolonged bed rest · Age-related baroreflex dysfunction.

Clinical assessment

Symptoms: dizziness, light-headedness, blurred vision, presyncope or syncope on standing. Diagnosis: measure supine BP, then standing BP at 1, 3, and 5 minutes. A drop ≥20/10 mmHg confirms the diagnosis.
